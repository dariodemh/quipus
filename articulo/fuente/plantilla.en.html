What the khipus add up to

A reading of an Inka accounting archive and a statistical analysis of every digitized khipu

dario, with AI agentsAbout 45 minutes to readCode and dataReferences ↓Leer en español

The short version

At the Inka storehouse of Inkawasi, in the Cañete valley, archaeologists found fourteen khipus piled together and covered with peanuts. We propose a reconstruction, figure by figure, of the accounts they kept, and we leave it open for others to check.

On our reading, nine units (possibly pachakas, a unit of Inka social organization) delivered a product, probably peanuts, and each one contributed two parts. One khipu recorded the two parts of each unit in each delivery. Another copied those figures out clean into a table of nine columns, one per unit. The row totals of that table appear later, in the same order, in a summary ledger of which two copies survive: eleven exactly and seven with differences, five of them off by one; for the rest, the destination has not survived. And a pair of tied khipus reaches several of those same totals by another route, adding up the parts instead of the units.

From the ten khipus that enter the account we have 1461 figures, and for about 1100 of them we know what role they play. If the reading is correct, one can follow a number from the cord where it was recorded to the ledger where it ended up, predict missing cords and point to knots that may have been miscounted. An AI agent that worked blind, without the khipu names, found on its own the first stage of the chain (UR270 to UR278) and did not find the link from UR278 to the ledger. It builds on the work of Gary Urton and Alejandro Chu, who had already seen at Inkawasi pairs of khipus with similar figures and fixed quotas that were subtracted from each delivery.

The rest of the work looks at the 650 khipus of the digitized corpus (the database has 651; one is a duplicate), with almost 58,000 cords. Seen as a whole, the khipus we can read look less like a text and more like what we would do today with a spreadsheet. They are tables of rows and columns, forms that always split an amount in the same proportions, lists with their totals and registers of people. Some archives had their own formats, and documents were copied, summarized one another and kept together, as in a system of account books.

These are the findings. Each card leads to its section.

None of the labels means that something is proven. They say how well each result held up under the tests we ran, and each one is in the repository for others to check. In the repository and in the reader's output the earlier names are used: “well supported” for strong indication, “supported” for indication and “proposed” for conjecture.

Our data also do not support some earlier proposals, such as that most khipus have recognizable internal sums, that colors had a shared meaning across the whole empire, or that some khipus hold syllables. We explain this in a separate section, along with what it would take for the data to support them.

None of us, neither the person in charge of the project nor the agents, has seen the objects. Everything comes from published transcriptions, and so each finding ends with what one would have to look at in a museum or an archive to confirm or reject it. The code, the data and a khipu reader that gives the profile of any khipu in the corpus, or of a new one, are in a public repository.

What a khipu is

A khipu is a primary cord from which other cords hang, sometimes hundreds. The pendant cords carry knots, and smaller cords, the subsidiaries, can hang from them. The cords have colors, are twisted one way or the other, and are attached to the primary cord at the front or at the back.

In the absence of alphabetic writing, khipus were used to record censuses, tribute, and what went in and out of the state storehouses. The specialists who made and read them were called khipukamayuq. After the conquest they remained in use for decades, in some isolated communities into the 20th century, and some Spanish chroniclers transcribed what the khipukamayuq read to them.

How the numbers are read was shown in 1912 by Leland Locke, who confirmed on real khipus what the chronicler Garcilaso had already reported. He found that on some khipus a top cord was worth exactly the sum of the cords it grouped.

“The top cord sums the numbers on the pendent cords through which it is looped, thus giving an accurate key to the numerical character of the knots.”L. Leland Locke, 1912, p. 330

Since Locke it has been accepted that the system is decimal and positional. Thousands go near the primary cord, hundreds further down, then tens, and units at the end. Hundreds and tens are marked with clusters of single knots. Units are marked with a long knot of two to nine turns, and the number of turns is the digit; one, which cannot be made as a long knot, is a figure-eight knot. An empty space at a position is a zero.

That is, broadly, what is known with most confidence about how khipu numbers are read. It is not known what was being counted in each case, whether colors had a fixed meaning, or whether some khipus held anything besides numbers, such as names or stories. There are chronicles that speak of narrative khipus, but we know of no reading of one that has been generally accepted.

The data and how we worked

We used two public databases. The first is the Khipu Field Guide, edited and maintained by Ashok Khosla, in the version that accompanies the article by Medrano and Khosla (2024), with 651 khipus and 57,818 cords.1 The second is the Open Khipu Repository, which brings together transcriptions by several researchers. For each cord we have its knots, its value, its color, its length, which way it is twisted and how it is attached. Without the work of those who built these databases, this project would not have been possible.

The two databases agree on 99.4% of the knotted cords that can be compared. The differences are concentrated in five khipus, and half of them are errors by a factor of ten, a digit shifted by one position.

The analysis was done by artificial intelligence agents, coordinated by one person. The agents wrote and ran the programs. Some reviewed the work of others: one redid the Inkawasi relations from scratch without reading the earlier code, another audited the 74% figure, and a red team tried to refute the Inkawasi results and some of the others. The work has not yet gone through peer review. That is why we chose this continual contradiction between agents, and to leave everything, data and programs, in a public repository where anyone can correct or improve it. We invite specialists in khipus, Andean history and statistics to review it. Objections, corrections and, above all, new readings of the objects themselves can be sent as an issue in the repository.

No agent has had direct access to a khipu. Everything comes from published transcriptions and from the literature, and that lack of contact with the objects is the first thing that would need to be addressed in a next stage.

The problem of chance

With thousands of cords and thousands of numbers, coincidences always turn up. If you look for sums, you find sums, and if you look for syllables, you find those too. The question that matters is how many would turn up by chance.

To answer it we used permutation tests. The idea is simple. We shuffle the cord values many times, within the same khipu or the same group depending on the question, and count how many coincidences turn up in the shuffled data. If the real data have many more, there is something there that chance does not explain. If they have the same number, we cannot claim anything. The figure below does this on a small scale.

The probability we give for each result, the p, says how often chance would produce something equal or better. A p of 0.0002 means 2 times in 10,000. Because we ran many tests, we also correct for their number. If you run a hundred tests, five come out “significant” by sheer luck, and the Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg corrections adjust for that.2

Before using a method on something new, we tested it on something known. If a sum detector does not find the sums others have already published, it is no use. The main control was Puruchuco, a khipu archive near Lima where Gary Urton and Carrie Brezine showed in 2005 that some khipus summarized others. Our method found the same hierarchy and the two small discrepancies they pointed out, 2904 against 2908 and 161 against 162. In two other columns (1 and 16) the numbers do not match, and they do not mention it.3

When a test finds nothing

When a test finds nothing, one has to ask whether it could have found it. So, for several methods, we deliberately hid known patterns in test data and checked how often they were detected. If a method detects a real pattern only rarely, its not finding it says little. In those cases we write “not detectable with these data” and not “does not exist”.

A blind test

One of the tests was blind. We took the 52 khipus that the database attributes to Inkawasi, removed their names, hid 15% of the knotted cords (597) and asked an AI agent to guess the hidden values and look for relationships between khipus: sums between khipus, tables consolidated into other records, and fixed quotas. It was told not to consult the literature. We cannot show that web access was blocked in that run, and the model may have seen the literature during its training.4

The agent ventured to predict 68 hidden cords and got exactly 56 right, 82%. On its own it found the first stage of the chain (UR270 to UR278, adding each pendant to its subsidiary) and that UR275 repeats the totals of UR266. It did not find the link from UR278 to the ledger. That link was confirmed in a directed test on the anonymized data, whose family (tables of nine) we chose knowing it existed, with a rule that we gave it. With that blind reanalysis and a second program, the first stage moved from indication to strong indication; the test does not prove independence, given the caveat about the model.

How to read each finding

Each finding has the same four parts. What we found, how we tested it, how sure we are, and what is missing. This last part says which observation would strengthen it and, when we know, which would overturn it, and it is the most useful for anyone with access to the objects or the archives. When a finding supports, develops or adds evidence to an earlier proposal, we say so under a background heading.

Inkawasi, the storehouse

Inkawasi is in the Cañete valley, near Lunahuaná. The Inkas built it as a garrison and storage center during their war against the Guarco, who lived in the lower part of the valley. There the archaeologist Alejandro Chu excavated dozens of khipus, several covered with remains of chili peppers, peanuts and beans, which could be the products they recorded.

“Several of the khipus were found associated with, or covered by, aji (Capsicum sp.), peanuts (Arachis hypogaea), and black beans (Phaseolus vulgaris). We suggest that these may be the products that the khipus recorded.”Urton and Chu, 2015, abstract

Urton and Chu published in 2015 and 2018 that several of these khipus were tied together in pairs with similar figures, and that there were fixed amounts subtracted again and again, 10, 15, 47 and 208. They proposed two readings, one of them a quota separate from what was deposited, which in 2018 they developed as a tax. We checked the fixed amounts 47, 15 and 10, which is a result of theirs, and started from it.

The fourteen peanut khipus were piled in a corner of one of the storehouse's large halls.5 The first five findings come from them.

The findings

A reading of the peanut archive

Before going through it piece by piece, it helps to see the whole. Of the fourteen khipus in the pile, ten enter one and the same account: eight form the chain and two, UR269 and UR268, are side ledgers. Here it is, from the bottom up.

On our reading, at the lowest level, UR270 records each delivery in its nine-cord records as nine units, and each unit with two parts, one on the pendant cord and the other on its subsidiary. At the next level there are two summaries of the same detail. UR278 adds the two parts of each unit and builds a table of nine columns. The tied pair UR273A and UR273B instead adds all the parts of one kind and all those of the other. At the highest level, the ledger, of which two copies survive, UR275 and UR266, enters the total of each delivery, the fixed quota of 47 and the division of the rest. The two summaries reach the same ledger by different routes, as if the account were balanced by rows and by columns.

From those ten khipus we have 1461 figures. For about 1100 we know what role they play in the account, and each one carries in the repository its reading, its arithmetic link and its level of evidence. The closest work is that on Puruchuco, where Urton and Brezine (2005) showed that some khipus summarized others. Here we also try to follow each number along the whole chain, in both directions. We read in full Urton and Chu's work on Inkawasi, with its supplements, and Urton's 2017 chapter. We do not know whether other works on the site that we have not read anticipate part of this; “new”, here, means that it does not appear in what we read.

What we do not yet know is what the nine units were, what the two parts were, or what the peanuts were measured in. Findings 3 and 5 deal with those questions.

Finding 1Accounts on three levels

What we foundUR278 is a khipu arranged in groups of nine cords, and each group works as a row of a table with nine columns. UR270 is another khipu from the same pile, and in its nine-cord records each pendant cord has a subsidiary. If each cord of UR270 is added to its subsidiary, the result is the cells of UR278, column by column and in the same order.

In the first group of UR270, the first cord is worth 178 and its subsidiary 59, which add up to 237. The second is worth 200 and its subsidiary 95, which add up to 295. And so on for all nine.

The second step is to add up each row of UR278. Row 0 gives 415 + 415 + 353 + 415 + 415 + 415 + 414 + 413 + 363 = 3618, and that 3618 is one of the headers of UR266, a khipu that works as a summary ledger. Eleven row totals of UR278 appear as headers of UR266, in the same order. UR275, the other copy of the same ledger, has seven in the same order. The figure below links each row to its destination. That the lines do not cross is what “in the same order” means.

BackgroundDevelops observations by Urton (2017) and by Urton and Chu: that UR278 is organized in nines and that UR266 and UR275 closely match. It is a hierarchy of the same kind as the one Urton and Brezine showed at Puruchuco.

How we tested itIn five of the twelve comparable groups, between six and nine of the nine cells match, and with pendant plus subsidiary chance produces five groups like that 2 times in 10,000. If only the pendant cords are used, without the subsidiary, no group reaches six. In the step to the ledger, by chance one would expect less than one header in the right order, and there are eleven. A second program, written separately, found the same thing. The agent in the blind test found the first stage on its own; the step to the ledger was confirmed in a directed test on the anonymized data, whose family (tables of nine) we chose knowing it existed, with a rule that we gave it.

With the rules of the chain, cords can be predicted. If one cord at a time is hidden and calculated from the others, the prediction is exactly right for 59% of the cords the chain allows us to predict, against 21% for the best alternative without the chain.

How sure we areStrong indication, for the arithmetic of the chain. From UR270 to UR278 it rests on a second program, sharing no code with the first, and on the blind reanalysis; from UR278 to the ledger, on that second program and on the directed test. There is a limit worth stating. On the cords we did not use to discover the rules, the predictive advantage almost disappears, 18% against 15%. What holds up best is the arithmetic of the chain, not everything each khipu says.

What is missingA rereading of the khipus by someone else. For Inkawasi, the two databases come from the same reading, and a second, independent reading is what this whole work lacks most.6 It would also help to find another nine-column table at Inkawasi, for chili peppers or beans. If it has the same profile as UR278 (finding 3), the nine units would not be specific to peanuts.

Finding 2A second route to the same ledger

What we foundIn the same pile there is a pair of tied khipus, UR273A and UR273B. If they are added record by record, eight of eighteen sums match headers in the ledgers. The table in UR278 adds by units and the pair adds by parts, and both arrive at the same totals.

The pair is completed by its copies. When a record of UR273A is blank, its copy UR274A takes its place, and the sum again gives the header in 3 of 4 cases. The copies also carry their own fixed quotas, 30 in one and 17 in the other, which together give the 47 in the ledger. This last point is only a proposal.

BackgroundAdds evidence to an observation by Urton and Chu, who noticed that the figures in UR273B resembled those in the ledgers.

How we tested itBy chance one would expect about one match, and there are eight. The result holds up when the pair is split into two halves and each is tested separately. It weakens when all the combinations of pair, header definition and destination that could be tried are counted,7 and in the blind test, with the complete data, it ranked among the top five of 132,600 possible trios, without passing the correction.

How sure we areIndication The sum of the quotas is a conjecture.

What is missingAn independent rereading of UR273A, UR273B and UR274A. If seven of the eight sums or more hold, it is strengthened; if three or more break, it falls. There is also one specific cord, number 104 of UR266. Today it is worth 1737, which is exactly one of the sums, but Urton published it as 1732, and counting the turns of its last knot decides it. Finally, of the 34 khipus excavated at Inkawasi only 29 have been described. If there is another tied pair among the other five, we predict that its sums will give headers of another ledger in the archive, UR269.

Finding 3Nine fixed columns

What we foundThe columns of the table are always the same nine, in the same order, in UR270 and in UR278. They are also very even, because each column receives more or less the same in each row. But they are not equal. Overall they go down from largest to smallest, with the first two as the largest and the last two as the smallest, and those proportions hold from row to row.

What group of nine receives even but ordered amounts? It does not look like a population, because populations vary much more, nor a calendar, because there are no cycles. One possibility is that they are nine pachakas, the units of about a hundred households into which a waranga, of a thousand, was divided. We looked for colonial lists of seven or more units and compared nine. Of those, the one for the nine pachakas of the waranga of Bambamarca, in Cajamarca, is the only one that goes down with position as steadily as UR278, according to the partial 1568 copy of the 1567 visita given by Martti Pärssinen (1992). Bambamarca is quite a bit more unequal, and the two profiles correlate +0.64 (p = 0.07), suggestive but not significant.8

BackgroundDevelops Urton's (2017) observation that UR278 is organized in groups of nine.

How we tested itThere are 362,880 ways to order nine columns, and in the five records that can be compared, the same order is the one that best fits UR270 to UR278, alone or tied with others. To measure how even they are, we compared them with every group of nine cords in the corpus. Of the eight most even groups of nine cords among those that add up to more than 500, seven are in UR278. The order from largest to smallest was tested against chance, and the tendency to decrease with position is not significant after correction (Spearman −0.73, q = 0.08). The proportions calculated from half of the rows resemble those of the other half, although not significantly (p = 0.06). That a list of nine pachakas goes down from largest to smallest as much as Bambamarca's happens by chance about 14 times in 1000, corrected for the nine lists tested.

How sure we areThat the nine columns have fixed, unequal shares (Friedman test), indication. The decreasing trend with position is not significant after correction. The resemblance to Bambamarca and the reading as pachakas, conjectures.

What is missingThree things in the archives. Look at the 1568 copy of the Bambamarca visita, which is the one Pärssinen uses, and confirm that the pachakas appear in that order; if they appear in another, the resemblance is the editor's doing and it falls. Find a list of pachakas for Lunahuaná or the Guarco, which we do not know of today; if some waranga in the valley had nine pachakas of moderately unequal sizes, the idea is greatly strengthened. And check other colonial lists of nine units read from khipus, because if many go down from largest to smallest, the resemblance to Bambamarca is no longer special.

Finding 4The accountants' mistakes

What we foundIn the step from UR278 to the ledger, of the 18 rows that have a destination, 11 match exactly and 7 do not. These are the differences.

+6 · +100 · +1 · −1 · +1 · −1 · −1

They do not all go the same way. If they were a rule, like rounding or deducting something, they would have the same sign or the same size. Six of the seven fall in the units, which are marked with the long knot, and that is what one expects if someone miscounted the turns of a knot.

One case serves as an example. The first cell of row 21 of UR278 is cord 180. It has two knots in the hundreds, three in the tens, a long knot of seven turns and a figure-eight knot, and it adds up to 238. UR270 gives 237 for that cell. Also, row 21 adds up to 1654 and the ledger says 1653. With six turns instead of seven, both accounts would balance. A single knot could explain both differences. Try it.

The two copies of the ledger, UR266 and UR275, repeat the same value in the five mismatched rows that have a counterpart in both (rows 8, 12, 13, 19 and 21; values 3249, 2633, 3096, 2106 and 1653). That is what one expects if both come from a single source that already carried those differences, whether a lost khipu or one of them copied from the other. We cannot tell them apart.

How we tested itWe compared where the differences fall with where they would fall if the errors were spread at random across the knot positions. Six of seven falling in the units happens by chance about 7 times in 1000. There are few cases, so the figure is approximate.

How sure we areIndication, with few cases. That there was a lost khipu, and not a copy of one by the other, is a conjecture.

What is missingCounting turns on the object. We predict that the long knot of cord 180 has six turns; if it has seven and is intact, the difference was already in the original record. The same goes for rows 13, 17, 19 and, with reservations because its destination is doubtful, 29. The difference of +6 in row 8 is of another kind, and there we predict that cord 77 is missing a long knot of six turns, because it is the only one in its row with no units knot.

Finding 5How much was stored

What we foundIf the khipus count produce, we still need to know in what unit. There are two candidates that the data do not separate. The fanega is the Castilian measure, of about 56 liters (LeVine 1992 gives “about 1.6 bushels per hanega”; an editor's note in Murra (2024) gives about 55.5 liters for Castile). The carga is what a person or a llama carried, and its size is unclear: LeVine (1992) warns that “the precise quantity of a carga is unclear”. In the Charcas transcriptions studied by Platt (2002), the same delivery appears as 250 fanegas in one transcription and as 500 cargas in another, and he glosses cargas as llama loads; there, then, a carga is half a fanega, about 28 liters. LeVine also reports a man carrying “half an hanega of maize (about 21.8 kg)”.

For peanuts in the shell, the measured bulk density is 0.286 kg per liter (Shukla, Makwana and Gupta 2019, pods of one modern variety). One unit of peanuts in the shell would then weigh about 8 kg if it is a carga and about 16 kg if it is a fanega. For the UR278 table, about 59,000 units in its 26 complete rows, that gives about 470 tonnes or about 950: an order of magnitude of roughly 500 to 950 tonnes. There are two reservations. The equivalence of two cargas per fanega comes from sixteenth-century Charcas, not Cañete, and nothing in the khipus says that the unit was either one.

BackgroundUrton and Chu raised the possibility of standardized accounting units for agricultural produce (in their text, “possible standardized accounting units”). Here they are given a weight, with the reservations above.

How we tested itWe converted the UR278 total to weight with each possible unit and compared it with the capacity of the Inkawasi storehouses. The capacity only rules out very small units.

How sure we areConjecture Our first figure, 1390 tonnes, rested on a load of 23 kg for which we found no source. With units that do have a source, the order of magnitude is 500 to 950 tonnes, and the weight per liter comes from the pods of a single modern variety.

What is missingAn independent piece of data on the unit, such as a colonial document saying what deliveries were measured in in the area, or the measurements of one of the halls where the produce was stored.

Beyond Inkawasi

The findings that follow come from looking at the 650 khipus at once, searching for relationships between khipus and for formats that recur.

Finding 6Copies from one museum to another

What we foundThe khipus that survived were scattered across museums in Europe and the Americas. We found 25 pairs of copies. Some had already been published and others do not appear in the literature we reviewed. HP055, which is in Lima, and UR1084, which is in Paris, share 121 of 142 aligned cords with the same value (HP055 has 162 knotted cords and UR1084 has 258). Chirinos (2010) had already reported that a Paris khipu, AS84 (UR1084 in our data), has a copy of its first 15 groups in a Lima museum, which he saw in 2009, and HP055 fits his description. What we add is the cord-by-cord measurement, the test against chance and the search across the whole corpus.

The data suggest that copying was an archival practice. Within the same site, the probability that two khipus are copies of each other is almost 18 times higher than between sites. In three archives we know to be nearly complete, Inkawasi, Puruchuco and Leymebamba, 8 of 52 khipus (counting copies found after the first search), 4 of about 20 and 3 of 22 have a copy.

The copies are of several kinds. Some are duplicates; others copy only a stretch or break down the figures differently; and there are extracts. UR1104, which is in Berlin and comes from Pachacamac, copies in order the headers of nine records of another khipu, UR234. Seven are exact, one has two digits swapped, 3102 for 3012, and the sixth repeats a header that is broken in UR234 today. If it is an extract, whoever made it also copied the cord that is now damaged and, if the copy is faithful, preserves what it said.

BackgroundExtends Urton's (2005) work on duplicate accounts: khipus paired within a single museum or site (Berlin, New York, Puruchuco, Chachapoyas), which he linked to Garcilaso's account that several khipukamayuq kept the same records. None of his pairs is between different museums. Chirinos (2010) described two copies between museums: the Lima copy of the Paris khipu (p. 320) and the Gothenburg copy of KH0049 (pp. 233, 339 and 341; finding 7). He also discusses UR1104, which he calls AS104 (pp. 249–251), with a different reading, as a yupana riddle, and does not relate it to UR234; in 2026 he himself considered that reading flawed (Chirinos 2026, note 17). Here it is measured across the whole corpus and between museums.

How we tested itWe compared each khipu with all the others, looking for stretches of repeated values in the same order, with a method that comes from DNA sequence alignment. Each pair was tested against chance and corrected for the enormous number of possible pairs. For HP055 and UR1084 the probability by chance is practically zero, and their cord lengths are only weakly alike (a correlation of 0.31 on the 21 aligned cords that have a length in both; that is few), which supports their being two different objects and not the same khipu recorded twice, although this still needs checking on the objects.9

How sure we areCopies within a single archive were already published (Urton 2005; Urton and Chu). Copies between museums, indication, because physical identity and provenance still need to be verified. Copying as an archival practice, indication. UR1104 as an extract, indication, although we found it by looking at the data and it needs confirming.

What is missingFor the museums to confirm that each pair are physically distinct objects, with different lengths and twists. And in Berlin, to look at the broken header of UR234. We predict it is worth 2016, the number the extract preserves.

Finding 7A three-level table already described, and its copy

What we foundKH0049, which is in the Museo Nacional in Lima, reads as a three-level table: 108 pendant cords in six blocks of 18. This structure was already described. The Aschers' Databook (1978, pp. 217–218), where the khipu appears as AS38, says that groups 1 and 2 are sums of groups 3 to 6 (group 2 is the sum of groups 4, 5 and 6, and group 1 that of groups 2 and 3) and notes the discrepancies, among them that the sums on the first cord of groups 1 and 2 are off by 3 in 27,000 and 15,000. Chirinos (2010) identified a khipu in Gothenburg, UR122, as a partial copy of KH0049; in our data it copies, with differences, four of the six blocks.

This finding is therefore a check that our method recovers a published structure, as at Puruchuco. What we add is the test against chance (one block is the sum of two others in 14 of 18 positions, and one of those is the sum of three others in 7 of 18) and the observation that the Gothenburg copy carries the same 15652 where the sum gives 15649, so the discrepancy was already in the source.

BackgroundThe hierarchy and its discrepancies are in the Aschers' Databook (1978, AS38, observations 6 and 7, pp. 217–218); the copy is in Chirinos (2010, pp. 233, 339 and 341), who identified UR122 as a partial copy of this khipu: the same values in the same positions, with fewer groups and without the color differences of the original. What we add is the test against chance and the discrepancy that the two copies share. The Khipu Field Guide already listed some sums in KH0049.

How we tested itWe shuffled the positions of one summand within its block, 20,000 times. The most that turned up was 6 of 18 for the first sum and 4 of 18 for the second; no shuffle matched what we observed (p < 0.0001).

How sure we areIndication, for what we add, the test against chance. The structure itself is published.

What is missingIn Gothenburg, to see whether the cords of UR122 that are worth zero today are broken or loose, as we predict. In Lima, to reread cord 19 of KH0049 and confirm that it is worth 15652, as the data show.

Finding 8Tables written with two alternating colors

What we foundOn some khipus the cords alternate two colors regularly, A, B, A, B. We read this as a two-column table written interleaved, with one column per color. There are 19 khipus out of 515 where color repeats every two cords more than chance would give (in some, more than two colors are involved), and in two of them, UR040 and UR167, the pattern is very clear: 26 of 31 consecutive A, B, A trios and 30 of 35.

How we tested itWe shuffled the colors of the cords and measured how often a two-color pattern repeated. We first looked for it in half of the khipus and confirmed it in the other half, which we had not looked at. Among 180 khipus that had no known structure, it was the only new structure that passed that confirmation.

How sure we areThe format in UR040 and UR167, indication: it was found in one half of the khipus and confirmed in the other. Their values are almost all small, so we see how the table is put together but not what it records.

What is missingAnother reading of UR040, at the Peabody, and of UR167, at the American Museum of Natural History, confirming the same colors in the same positions.

Finding 9Each khipu has its own color key

What we foundWithin each khipu, color seems to work as formatting: it marks the column, the group or the total cord, like cell formatting in a spreadsheet. In 165 of 222 khipus, color follows cord position much more than chance would give. But what each color means changes from one khipu to another. Each khipu seems to bring its own key, like a table whose legend has not survived.

BackgroundAdds evidence to the idea, already suggested in the literature, that colors worked as local keys. Chirinos (2010, p. 233) argued for it from the copies between museums: if color had a fixed meaning, the copies would have to keep the color differences, and they do not; he proposed that color served to give elegance and make the khipu easier to read. We found no earlier measurement against chance across the whole corpus, in what we read.

How we tested itBy chance one would expect about 20 khipus out of 403 in which color goes with the size of the number, and there are 167; with a chance model that respects the cord groups, 103 remain. But that signal is one of position: comparing the same position in different groups leaves 10 of 205, the level of chance. To see whether a color means the same thing on different khipus, we compared color orders and sequences between khipus and between sites, and they gave no more matches than chance.

How sure we areThat color marks column or group within each khipu, indication. That it also separates categories on its own, conjecture. On the lack of a shared code, see the section on proposals.

What is missingColor names depend on who transcribes. UR1107 and UR237 turned out to be the same khipu recorded twice. The values match on 23 of the 26 knotted cords, and the other three differ: in two it is the same knot at another decimal position (100 against 10, 50 against 500) and in one the final knot differs (60 against 66). The color labels do not match on any cord.

A rereading of colors with a common standard, or with calibrated photographs, would allow the comparison between khipus to be repeated with more confidence.

Finding 10Recurring ways of building a table

What we foundThere are formats that recur on khipus from different places. The top cord adds up the group immediately before it. A row of cords is followed by a separate cord with its total. And the largest cord opens the group.

BackgroundDevelops what Locke observed, that top cords add up others. Here we see that they add up the preceding group and not just any group. Chirinos (2010, pp. 237–238 and 297–305) had already grouped several khipus with top cords, among them B8713, AS199, UR118 and UR119, as a type with interrelated sums and a predominance of groups of six.

How we tested itThe top cord adds up the preceding group in 23 of 72 cases in the sample of top cords already curated by the Khipu Field Guide, when chance would give 2 or 3. The row with a separate total appears 51 times against 15 expected in 49 evaluable khipus; the signal is concentrated in seven (without them, 14 against 7). The largest cord opens the group in 26% of groups, against 15% expected, not counting Inkawasi.

How sure we areThe first two, indications on the khipus where they were measured, though that says nothing about how common they are in the corpus. The third, indication.

What is missingA finer search for precedents, and testing the formats on khipus that are not in the databases.

Finding 11A form with a fixed split

What we foundAt the Banco Central de Reserva del Perú there are five tied khipus, UR053A to UR053E. Each one splits its amounts into cords of fixed colors, one white, one red and, in most, one of a third color, which in UR053B and UR053E hangs from the red one, or from the red one's cord, as a subsidiary. The split stays close to 4 to 1 to 1 in all five, although their totals range from 1200 to 36,800.

BackgroundDevelops the description by Urton (2017), who had already noticed the same color vocabulary on the five khipus. Chirinos (2026), in a post on the Khipu Field Guide blog, described that all five have groups of three cords that repeat the color series W, RL and AB, that the W cords have the highest values “with very few exceptions”, and that in several of the khipus the parts keep “approximate proportions” to one another in the W and RL cords, with AB out of proportion in three of them. He reads the set, as a hypothesis, in terms of political entities (ayllu, pachaka, waranqa and hunu). For him, the meaningful sums are those of one color across the groups and not that of the three cords of a group, because they could be three different goods or services. What we add is narrower: the share of the total of each khipu that goes to each color (W between 0.57 and 0.70, about 4 to 1 to 1) and the fact that this share holds with totals from 1200 to 36,800. It is a description of the numbers, not evidence that there was a single allocation rule.

How we tested itIn UR053D, the white cord is the largest of the trio in 24 of 24 trios, and in UR053A, in 13 of 14. In both cases, chance does not get there.

How sure we areIndication, as a description of the numbers. That it is a form with a stable splitting key, a second case independent of Inkawasi, is a conjecture.

What is missingA rereading of the colors on all five, because if the red turned out to be two different colors with different splits, the result changes. And a search at the Banco Central for other khipus from the same find. Nor have we tested the sum Chirinos (2026) reports between part IV of UR053E and four groups of UR053B or UR053C.

Finding 12Khipus as a system of records

What we foundSeen as a whole, the khipus we can read look less like a text and more like what we would do today with a spreadsheet. They are tables of rows and columns, forms that always split an amount in the same proportions, lists with their totals and registers of people. Some archives had their own formats. On our reading, documents were copied, summarized one another and kept together, as in a system of account books. The earliest accounting archives of Mesopotamia (Nissen et al., 1993) have these same features, with hierarchies of documents, fixed formats for each type of record, and copies, although there what was copied were lists of words, not accounts.

BackgroundThat numerical khipus recorded quantities has been known since Locke. In 2026 Maria Contreras grouped khipus by their structural features and found that the groups relate in part to provenance, although the corpus carries the bias of collecting. This finding agrees with hers and adds a classification by the function each khipu serves.

How we tested itKhipus behave more like data than like text. In a text, the longer it is, the more new words appear, at a rate measured by the Heaps exponent: 0.90 in a Quechua text split into syllables. On khipus, by color, the median exponent is 0.44: the repertoire runs out much earlier, as in a table where the same values repeat. To classify the corpus we wrote explicit rules for each type of record and checked that they recognize eight khipus of known type, eight out of eight. Then we shuffled the features between khipus (200 repetitions) to see whether the types depended on the site of origin: the association between type and provenance is larger than in every shuffle (p < 0.005).10

How sure we areThat they behave like data and that each archive had its own formats, indication. The comparison with a spreadsheet or with a system of account books is an analogy, a conjecture.

What is missingToday we can recognize the type of document for a third of the khipus, which hold half of the cords. Another 23% have a probable type, but with low confidence. 28% are short lists of numbers in which these methods detect no structure, and which could be anything. The remaining 17% do not have enough data. Each new khipu that is transcribed, once run through the khipu reader, will tell whether it fits one of the types or whether a new one is needed.

The full table in the repository has more than 60 findings and corrections, each with its statistic and the file that produces it.

Proposals these data do not support

Khipus have been studied for more than a century, and there are many proposals about what they contain. We put some of them to the test with the same method, comparing with chance, and in several what is observed in the data looks like what chance produces on its own. That does not mean the proposal is false. It means these data do not support it, and in each case we say what it would take for them to do so.

Some of these proposals come from the same researchers whose databases we use. Their work is the starting point for ours.

How many khipus have sums

In 2024, Medrano and Khosla published that at least 74% of khipus have some of the relationships the Aschers described, almost always internal sums, cords whose value is the sum of others.

“We find that Ascher formulae characterize at least 74% of khipus, which exhibit meaningful arrangements of internal sums.”Medrano and Khosla, 2024

The count is done correctly, although not all of that 74% are sums: 447 khipus, 69%, have them between cords, and another 35 come in through relationships of another kind. Medrano and Khosla themselves asked how many of those sums would turn up by chance. They compared the corpus with khipus simulated in three ways. In the discussion of the article, what they point to as different from chance is not the 74% but other features: in the real corpus the sum cord sits more often on one side of its summands than on the other, 54 against 46, while in the simulated ones it sits close to 50 and 50, and the simulated sums have fewer summands. We reproduce that asymmetry (53.9%, p < 0.002), although about half of it is explained by where the large values sit. We repeated their question khipu by khipu, shuffling each khipu's own values. A long khipu, with many numbers, has a huge number of possible combinations, and some add up by sheer chance. When the values are shuffled, between 93% and 100% of the sums that measure detects still turn up, depending on the shuffle and the type of sum.

That does not rule out that khipus have sums, nor does it contradict what Medrano and Khosla argue. It is a precision about how the 74% figure is usually read: the measure, on its own, does not separate sums from chance. We estimate that, even with a real sum in every group of every khipu, the measure would detect it in only 24% of khipus. With a stricter test, 14 khipus have sums that withstand two different shuffles, and nine of them are from Inkawasi.

What would make it informativeA measure of sums that is compared with chance khipu by khipu.

A shared color code

Since the chronicles, it has been assumed that colors had fixed meanings, shared by all khipukamayuq. Our finding 9 says that within each khipu color does mark columns and groups. But when we compare, across khipus from unrelated archives, which cords share the same color pattern, the match (3.1%) is that of chance (3.0%).

What would rescue itColors recorded with a common standard. UR1107 and UR237 is the only case we have of one khipu transcribed twice with colors in both, and there the labels agree on none of the 23 cords, so a shared code could be hidden behind that noise.

Khipus that hold syllables

Some recent works propose that certain khipus record words, syllables or names. The most recent is a preprint by Julien Sivan, from March 2026. We tested the idea in several ways. As we saw in finding 12, khipus behave more like data tables than like texts. Khipus without numbers have a median of five colors and a maximum of 19 (39 without grouping shades), whereas Hyland (2017) gives 80 to 800 signs for logosyllabic systems. And the predictions Sivan's proposal makes about its own examples are not borne out in these data, except for UR144.11

What would rescue itA khipu with a repertoire of signs much larger than the known ones. The Collata letters that Sabine Hyland (2017) studied have 95 distinct signs, combining color, fiber and twist, and no non-numerical khipu in the corpus goes beyond 39 distinct colors.

Calendars

It has been proposed that some khipus record calendars. We looked for calendrical numbers (365, 360, months of 29 or 30 days and others) in totals, groups and cord counts, in 111 tests. Five came out positive, the same as expected by chance. One specific case, UR006, has 24 very regular groups, as Urton (2017) described, and its reading as a calendar remains possible, but the numbers alone do not prove it.

What would rescue itA khipu whose groups match a known calendar better than chance, with a rule fixed before looking at it.

The Santa census

Urton proposed (2017) and Medrano and Urton developed (2018) that six khipus from the Santa valley correspond to a 1670 colonial census of San Pedro de Corongo. The sum of the first cords of the groups gives almost exactly the tribute in the census, but the assignment was chosen post hoc and the support is weak. FitzPatrick proposed in 2024 how to split the census's six pachakas into two moieties. With the correct null the probability of a fit like this by chance is 0.24, and the order of the census names cannot be distinguished from chance.

What would rescue itA test of the alignment with a rule fixed in advance, or more khipus from the same lot.

A system without zero

That an empty space at a position is a zero has been accepted since Locke. Florio and Overmann (2025) question it: they doubt that the system had a zero and was positional, and conclude that Inka numbers were “likely non-positional” (p. 128). For them the blank space is a consequence of using cord and knots (p. 136), and a zero conceived as a number would have had its own knot (p. 137).

This is our measurement: 726 cords with a zero in the middle, with 727 gaps. The gap measures almost exactly what a position with knots does: measured in bands, the median is 0.96, and within a single cord the interval that skips the zero measures 1.89 times the spacing between two adjacent positions (201 cords). What this says is limited: the gap is as wide as a position, which is what a positional layout produces. It bears on the argument that the blank is a by-product of the medium, and not on the argument for a dedicated knot for zero. We do not say that their proposal is refuted.

There is a caveat. The transcription assigns values to knots partly according to their position, so the measurement is not entirely independent of what it measures.

What would rescue itThat, measured on the objects, the gaps do not correspond to a decimal position. We predict that the gap of a zero measures between 0.8 and 1.1 times the width of a position.

A proposal of our own that failed

We also tested ideas of our own. We suggested that two khipus, UR050 and UR055, were a pair that recorded ceques, the ritual lines that radiated out from Cusco. We wrote three predictions, none held, and we withdrew it.

What we corrected along the way

We were wrong several times and we wrote it all down, because a hidden error takes value away from everything else. Some examples.

  • Saying that the 74% “cannot be distinguished from chance” was more than we could claim. What we say is that the measure is not informative.
  • The relationship of UR278 to UR266 gave 16, 15 and 11 matches depending on the criterion for what counted as a match; 11 is the figure a second program reproduced.
  • The pair UR273A and UR273B dropped from strong indication to indication. Another pair that we thought added up to a third khipu did not pass the test with new data and we withdrew it.
  • The fixed quotas and the copies at Inkawasi are Urton and Chu's, not a finding of ours.
  • A match with the Santa census gave us a probability of 0.006, but the calculation was circular. Done properly it gives 0.24.
  • The first tonnage figure, 1390 tonnes, rested on a load of 23 kg for which we found no source; with units that do have one, the order of magnitude is 500 to 950 tonnes.
  • Finding 7 turned out to be already described by the Aschers in their Databook (1978); we now present it as a check of the method and not as a discovery. Four findings went down from strong indication to indication because they had no separate second analysis.

The full list is in the repository.

The khipu reader

Everything above was packaged into a program, the khipu reader (Khipu Reader). You give it the name of a khipu in the corpus, or a table with the cords of a new khipu, and it returns its reading profile.

The profile includes a physical summary, with how many cords, groups and colors the khipu has and how many are broken, and how its numbers are written. It says whether it has internal sums and whether there are more than chance would give, whether color marks groups or columns, and whether it follows any of the formats we found, such as the table with two alternating colors or the table with column totals. It shows which other khipus in the corpus it is related to, whether by copying or because its totals reappear in another, and which type of record it resembles. Each line states its level of evidence and which part of the repository it comes from. Here are four real profiles, exactly as the program gives them.

The reader runs over the 651 khipus of the corpus and has a battery of 19 checks that fails if any known result breaks. It also runs negative controls, khipus with shuffled values, to make sure it does not see things where there are none.

The reader does not interpret or say what was being counted. It describes the structure of a khipu and the confidence each statement deserves. We designed it above all for anyone who transcribes a new khipu and wants to know within minutes whether it resembles anything known.

How it was made

This work was done by artificial intelligence agents (Claude (Opus and Sonnet) and DeepSeek (Flash), in separate tasks), directed by one person. The agents wrote the code and ran the tests, and some reviewed the work of others. A person decided what to ask, what to believe and what to publish. No one has yet peer-reviewed this work.

Everything is at github.com/dariodemh/quipus. One program checks the main results of the review package (15 checks) and another the reader (19), and they fail if anything changes. The full reading of the peanut archive, with its 1461 figures, is in the repository. The predictions for each finding, in more detail and with a few more, are in the repository. If any of them fails, we will say so. Reviews and corrections are welcome in the repository's issues.
